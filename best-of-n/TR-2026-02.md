# Best-of-N: Inference-Time Compute for Small Language Models

**Technical note TR-2026-02**
Alejandro Areces Rivera · Interlace AI · August 2026

Supersedes TR-2026-01 (`10.5281/zenodo.21936833`). See §8 for what changed and
why.

---

## Abstract

A language model does not return an answer; it returns a sample from a
distribution over answers. We measure what happens when a frozen model is
sampled 128 times per problem and the result is selected rather than taken as
given. On GSM8K, `Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct` goes from **45.1%** at a single sample
to **66.5%** under majority voting, with no weights modified. Coverage — whether
any trajectory reaches the correct answer — reaches **93.5%**.

We separate the gain into the part attributable to selection (**+20.1 points**)
and the part attributable to simply having a trajectory that finished (**+1.3
points**), a decomposition that is not novel in itself -- the coverage and
selection split appears in Snell et al. (2024) and in evaluation harnesses that
expose `pass@k` beside `maj@n` -- but which is rarely reported alongside a
headline, and which changes how single-sample baselines should be read. We also show that the
second term is a function of the experimental setup rather than of the method:
at a tighter token budget it was four times larger, and it would have been
reported as method gain. All 25,600 trajectories are published in full, and
every figure here is recomputed from them by a script that re-extracts answers
from the raw reasoning text.

## 1. The decomposition

Performance of a Best-of-N system factors into two quantities that need
different remedies:

- **Coverage**, `1 − (1−p)^N` — did any of the N trajectories reach the correct
  answer?
- **Selection** — did the system return it?

Coverage grows with N and saturates logarithmically. Selection does not improve
on its own. Measuring them separately tells a practitioner which of the two to
spend the next hour on, and that is the practical contribution of this work.

The framing is not new: it appears independently in Snell et al. (2024), in the
oracle-gap literature of 2026, and in evaluation harnesses that expose `pass@k`
alongside `maj@n`. What we add is the measurement discipline around it and the
data to check it.

## 2. Why voting recovers minority answers

Majority voting works better than its simplicity suggests, and the reason is an
asymmetry in how errors distribute.

A wrong trajectory has many available ways to be wrong and selects a different
one on each sample; errors therefore fragment into singletons. A correct
trajectory has one place to land. Over N samples the correct answer accumulates
mass while the incorrect ones divide it, so the mode can be correct even when
correct trajectories are a minority of the pool.

This holds only when answers are comparable — which makes answer extraction a
load-bearing component rather than a detail. §7 returns to this.

## 3. Method

| | |
|---|---|
| Model | `Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct`, weights frozen |
| Benchmark | GSM8K test split, 200 problems, seed 20260815 |
| Sampling | N = 128, temperature 0.7, top-p 0.95, 1024 max tokens |
| Prompt | Problem plus a request to reason step by step and box the answer |
| Hardware | One RTX 3090, vLLM backend, ~25 minutes |
| Software | `bestofn` 1.1.3 |

The 128 trajectories per problem were generated once. Results for smaller N are
obtained by subsampling that pool 200 times at each N, which yields the curve
and its spread without additional generation.

Answers are extracted from the last `\boxed{...}`, canonicalised, and compared
symbolically where `math-verify` is available. **A trajectory with no
recoverable answer abstains** rather than contributing the last number found in
its text.

## 4. Results

| N | random | majority | 95% CI | coverage |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 45.1% | 45.1% | [39.0, 52.0] | 45.1% |
| 2 | 46.0% | 46.4% | [38.0, 51.5] | 56.5% |
| 4 | 46.5% | 53.1% | [44.5, 57.5] | 66.7% |
| 8 | 46.0% | 58.3% | [53.0, 66.5] | 75.0% |
| 16 | 46.3% | 61.7% | [55.5, 69.0] | 81.7% |
| 32 | 46.1% | 64.1% | [57.5, 70.5] | 86.7% |
| 64 | 46.3% | 65.4% | [59.0, 72.0] | 90.6% |
| **128** | 46.4% | **66.5%** | [60.5, 73.0] | **93.5%** |

Intervals are 95% percentile bootstrap over problems, 2000 resamples.

**Significance.** Comparing majority voting against random selection at N=128
on the same problems, the median paired discordances over 200 random seeds are
44 (majority right, random wrong) against 4 (the reverse), for an exact McNemar
**p = 1.5 × 10⁻⁹**. The worst of the 200 seeds gives 43 against 11, **p = 1.4 ×
10⁻⁵**; the best gives 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁴.

**We quote the worst case.** `random` is a different draw on every seed, so a
p-value computed from one of them is a property of that seed as much as of the
data — ours moved across nine orders of magnitude while the conclusion did not
change once. A single figure presented as though it were a fixed quantity of
the dataset would be the more impressive number and the less honest one.

We report the paired test rather than a difference of percentages because the
two selectors run on identical trajectory pools; treating them as independent
samples discards the pairing and overstates the uncertainty.

### 4.1 Every selector against the baseline

| selector at N=128 | accuracy | 95% CI |
|---|---:|---:|
| `random` (mean of 200 seeds, sd 2.4) | 46.4% | [41.0, 55.0] |
| `majority` | **66.5%** | [60.5, 73.0] |
| `self_certainty` | 66.5% | [60.5, 73.0] |
| `oracle` | 93.5% | [90.0, 96.5] |

Weighting each vote by `exp(mean token log-probability)` lands exactly where
the unweighted vote does. That is worth stating because it is a negative result
about a method that sounds like it should help, and because it needs
log-probabilities that plain majority does not.

### 4.2 Where the gain comes from

| | accuracy | contribution |
|---|---:|---:|
| Single sample | 45.1% | |
| N=128, random among trajectories that answered | 46.4% | +1.3 |
| N=128, majority vote | 66.5% | **+20.1** |
| | | **+21.4 total** |

Random selection improves with N without performing any selection, because with
more trajectories at least one is likely to have produced a usable answer. A
single-sample baseline compared against an N-sample system absorbs that
improvement into the reported method gain.

Here the effect is small — 1.3 of 21.4 points — and 94% of the gain is genuine
selection. **The size of that term is a property of the experimental setup, not
of the method.** In the run behind TR-2026-01's successor draft, generated with
`max_tokens=400`, 20.8% of trajectories hit the limit and abstained; the same
decomposition gave +5.1 points to abstention avoidance out of +23.2, four times
the share reported here. Both runs would have produced a defensible-looking
headline. Only the decomposition distinguishes them.

### 4.3 Compute accounting

| | |
|---|---|
| Trajectories generated | 25,600 |
| Cast a vote | 24,797 (96.9%) |
| Abstained | 803 (3.1%) |
| Truncated at the token limit | 216 (0.8%), 215 of which abstained |
| Generated tokens | 8,434,157 |

Effective N is 124.0 rather than 128. Per-trajectory statistics computed
against the nominal N are biased low by about 3%.

The cost is linear in N: 128 trajectories cost 128 generations. On dedicated
hardware the marginal cost of the additional samples is idle GPU time; on
metered inference it is a multiplier on the bill. The trade is compute for
accuracy, and whether it is worth making depends entirely on which of those two
the reader is paying with.

## 5. Selection is invariant under permutation

Given a fixed pool of trajectories, the selected answer does not depend on the
order in which they are presented. This holds for every selector except
`random`, which is order-dependent by construction.

It is not automatic, and three distinct mechanisms had to be corrected for it.

*The partition.* Symbolic equivalence is not transitive: `math-verify` accepts
`0.3333333333` against `1/3`, and `1/3` against `0.33333333333333`, while
rejecting the two decimals against each other. Grouping greedily — comparing
each new answer against one representative per class and stopping at the first
match — yields a partition that depends on which answer the model emitted
first. We take the transitive closure over all pairs.

*The summation.* Weights accumulated with `+=` in list order make the total
depend on that order, because floating-point addition is not associative. Two
permutations can differ in the last bit, which is enough to flip a near-tie in
a confidence- or verifier-weighted vote. Totals are computed with `math.fsum`
over a key-sorted list, which is exactly rounded.

*The tie-break.* Exact ties resolve on the canonical key, not on which
trajectory arrived first. This applies to the argmax selectors as well as the
voting ones; `max` returns the first maximal element and so encodes arrival
order silently.

We verify the property two ways. Directly, by shuffling each of the 200
published pools eight times and confirming no returned answer moves. And in
the published output: at N=128 the resampled curve draws 128 trajectories from
a pool of 128, so every draw is a permutation of one pool, and the reported
standard deviation across those 200 draws is **0.00** — it was 0.25 under the
greedy grouping, and the exhausted-N row disagreed with the direct evaluation
by 0.3 points. The zero is a necessary condition rather than a sufficient one:
a permutation could in principle change a winner without changing whether it
was correct. The shuffle test is the sufficient check.

## 6. Reproduction

The published dataset contains, per trajectory: the complete generated text,
the extracted answer, the finish reason, the mean token log-probability, and
the token count. Per problem it records the question, the reference answer and
the prompt suffix.

**It does not record the sampling parameters or the library version.** Those
are stated in §3 and are reproducible from it, but they are not in the file,
and a reader should not have to take a table's word for what produced a
dataset. `scripts/run_gsm8k.py` writes them into every record as of 1.1.4; the
trajectories published here predate that and were regenerated before it landed.
The next run will carry them. Generation is not seeded — the trajectories are
reproducible as a distribution, not token for token — and that is a limitation
rather than a detail.

```bash
pip install bestofn
python scripts/analyse.py results/gsm8k_trajectories.jsonl
```

The script re-runs extraction over the raw text rather than reading the stored
answers. A replay that re-counts pre-extracted answers cannot detect an
extraction regression; this one reports drift between the stored and the
re-derived answer for every trajectory.

The reported drift is zero, and it is worth being precise about what that does
and does not establish. The stored answers were written by the same version of
the extractor that the replay runs, so zero is what a correct system must
report and not evidence in itself. The check earns its place against a *future*
version: run this replay under a later release and any change in extraction
behaviour shows up as a non-zero count, on this fixed corpus, before it reaches
a published number. It caught ten trajectories when the mixed-number defect in
§7 was corrected.

Roughly two minutes on a laptop CPU. No GPU is required.

## 7. Extraction as a load-bearing component

An extraction failure and a reasoning failure are indistinguishable at the
metric level, which makes extraction a place where errors hide rather than
announce themselves. Three behaviours are worth stating explicitly because all
three were wrong in an earlier version of this software:

**Structure must survive canonicalisation.** Collapsing `\frac{1}{2}` onto its
first digit makes one half, one third and minus one half compare equal. Deleting
commas turns the pair `(3,4)` into the integer `34` — not a lost answer but a
fabricated one, plausible enough to match a reference by accident. Case folding
has the same failure mode in a less obvious place: upper-casing `\frac` yields
a token no symbolic parser accepts, so the canonical form and the equivalence
test stop agreeing about the same answer.

**Juxtaposition is not always multiplication.** `2\frac{1}{2}` is two and a
half. Read by the general rule, where writing two expressions side by side
means multiplying them, it becomes 1. In this dataset ten trajectories were
affected; none of them happened to coincide with a gold answer, which is the
only reason the defect did not move the reported accuracy.

**Absence must be representable.** A trajectory truncated mid-reasoning
contains no answer, and neither does one that finished without boxing one, nor
one whose answer uses LaTeX this software cannot canonicalise — `\binom{5}{2}`
is 10, and stripping the command it does not understand yields the vote
`binom52`. Recovering "the last number in the text" in any of these cases
produces a ballot indistinguishable from a genuine one.

Here 803 trajectories abstain: 215 ran out of tokens and 588 finished normally
without leaving a usable answer -- most without a box at all, two carrying a
repeating decimal the extractor declines to guess at. At the tighter token
budget of the earlier run the truncation figure alone was 5,319 out of 25,600.

## 8. Relation to TR-2026-01

An adversarial audit of version 1.0.0 of the software found defects in
extraction, selection and memory management. The measurements reported in
TR-2026-01 were produced by that implementation and are not reproducible with
the corrected one. **They should not be cited.**

Specifically withdrawn: the claim of a trained outcome reward model achieving
ROC-AUC 0.910 and a 16.6-point improvement over majority voting. The model was
never published; the problem set on which the improvement was measured was
never published; and in the single evidence file that was published, the
verifier selected an answer identical to majority voting on all 30 problems,
for a net gain of zero.

The present release ships no reward model. It provides adapters for
third-party ones, with the range validation that prevents a reward model's
unbounded logits from silently degrading a weighted vote into an unweighted one.

The complete list of corrections is in the software changelog.

## 9. Limitations

The measurement is one model on one benchmark. Coverage saturating at 93.5%
says something about this model and these problems, not about inference-time
compute in general.

Coverage is a diagnostic, not a bound on realisable selectors: the problems
sustaining its tail are single correct answers among 128, which are never the
mode and cannot be identified without the reference answer. The distance
between 66.5% and 93.5% is therefore an upper bound on what better selection
could recover, not an estimate of it.
